Tree and geometry

The linear octree — keys, the forest of leaves, refinement and balance — and the map from a key to physical space.

Module

TreeAMR — Module
TreeAMR

A tree-based (octree-style) AMR discretization for Julia. Provides the mesh, the storage, and the inter-grid operations for block-structured adaptive mesh refinement — no physics.

See CODE.md in the package root for the full design document.

Tree core

TreeAMR.MortonKey — Type
MortonKey{D}

A key identifying one node of the forest: the root octree it belongs to, its refinement level, and its per-dimension integer coordinates at that level (each in 0:2^level-1).

Keys are totally ordered by isless: root index first, then Morton (Z-order) curve order, with an ancestor sorting before all of its descendants. The bit interleaving is computed on the fly from level and coords — there is no packed-integer representation, so the depth limit is MAX_LEVEL rather than 64 ÷ D.

Dimension 1 is the most significant dimension of the interleaving.

source
Base.isless — Method
isless(a::MortonKey{D}, b::MortonKey{D})

Curve order: by root index, then by Morton (Z-order) position, with an ancestor immediately preceding its descendants.

Rather than walking all MAX_LEVEL bit planes, this finds the dimension whose coordinates first differ at the highest bit and compares only that one — D cheap operations instead of 32D.

source
TreeAMR.MAX_LEVEL — Constant
MAX_LEVEL

The deepest refinement level a MortonKey can represent. Bounded by the UInt32 coordinate storage: a coordinate at level L runs over 0:2^L-1, so L cannot exceed 32.

Forests

TreeAMR.Forest — Type
Forest{D,T}

A D-dimensional brick of roots[1] × ... × roots[D] octree roots covering the rectangular physical domain extents, refined into a leaf-only linear octree.

  • leaves is the sorted (by MortonKey curve order) flat vector of the leaves that currently tile the domain. Only leaves carry data; they tile the domain exactly, with no overlap.
  • Refinement is all-or-nothing: a node is either a leaf or has exactly 2^D children. There is never a partially refined node.
  • periodic[d] selects whether dimension d wraps around the brick. Wraparound lives in the neighbor arithmetic, so periodic ghost filling needs no special-casing later.
  • reflecting[d] is a (lo, hi) pair selecting which faces of a non-periodic dimension are reflecting (M10): the solution beyond them is its own mirror image, with the parity each variable declares on its FieldSet. The tree does not see them — there is no neighbor across a reflecting face, as across any other non-periodic one — but the GhostSchedule does, and fills their ghosts by mirrored transfers.
  • outflow[d] is a (lo, hi) pair selecting the outflow faces of a non-periodic dimension: the solution beyond them repeats the nearest interior cell (zero-gradient extrapolation). Like a reflecting face it is the schedule's, filled by transfers with clamped rather than mirrored target rows, so it needs no hook and no parity, and runs on every backend and across ranks. A nonlinear condition, such as forbidding inflow, is the application's, applied to the ghosts after the fill. A face is reflecting or outflow, not both. The faces that are neither periodic, reflecting nor outflow are outer faces, and belong to the boundary hook of fill_ghosts!.
  • rotating = (d1, d2) declares a rotating seam (M12): only one quadrant of the (d1, d2) plane is simulated, and the other three are its images under quarter turns about the line where the low faces of d1 and d2 meet. The rotation R takes e_{d1} to e_{d2} and e_{d2} to -e_{d1}, so the order of the pair fixes its sense. Unlike a reflecting face, the tree sees the seam: the low face of d1 is glued to the low face of d2, neighbor_keys finds the real leaves across it, and balance! keeps the leaves on either side of it at one level (conformity). The two dimensions must have as many roots as each other and be neither periodic, reflecting nor outflow; their high faces stay outer. See "Rotating seams" in CODE.md.
  • halfturn = (d1, d2) declares a half-turn seam (M14) instead: only the half of the (d1, d2) plane above the low face of d1 is simulated, and the other half is its image under the rotation by 180° about the line where that face meets the centre plane of d2. The low face of d1 is glued to itself, reversed along d2; the tree sees it as it sees a quarter turn's seam, and keeps a leaf and its image at one level. roots[d2] must be even, so that the axis lies on a root boundary, and d1 and d2 must be neither periodic, reflecting nor outflow. A forest has one seam at most. See "Half turn" in CODE.md.
  • A forest may instead be several bricks — patches — glued at their faces (M17; the second form below), each with its own roots and its own logical extents. glue lists, for each glued face, the face across it and the signed axis permutation map that carries this patch's axes onto that patch's: map[e] = ±a sends axis e here to axis a there, reversed for a minus. Both directions of every gluing are listed, mutually inverse. The coordinates stay logical, patch by patch — the map from a patch's box to physical space is the application's — so patch extents may overlap or coincide, and patch and block_patches say which patch a block is in. Every patch has one cell size, and a glued face is neither periodic, reflecting nor outflow; a wrap is a patch glued to its own opposite face, so periodic is refused, and so is a seam. A step that leaves a patch through two glued faces at once, at a patch corner, finds no neighbor. No coarse-fine face crosses a glued face: balance! keeps the leaves on either side of one at one level (conformity), as across a seam. The root indices count through patch 1, then patch 2, and so on, and forest.roots and forest.extents are patch 1's. See "A multi-patch forest" in CODE.md.
  • N is the per-block interior size, and it is even: cells are the tree's geometry, so N belongs here. The ghost width G does not — it says how far a stencil reaches into a neighbor's data, which is a property of what is stored, and it belongs to the FieldSet (amended in M8; through M6 it was a forest keyword).
  • Blocks are cubes, so extents must match the aspect ratio of roots.
  • T is the floating-point type the geometry is computed in, not merely stored in — see floattype and "Precision" in CODE.md.

Root indices are linearized 0-based, dimension 1 fastest, over roots; see root_position and root_index.

Forest(roots; N, periodic=all false, reflecting=all false, outflow=all false,
       rotating=nothing, halfturn=nothing, extents=one unit per root,
       leaves=nothing, comm=nothing)
Forest{T}(roots; ...)                      # geometry in `T`
Forest(patches; N, glue, reflecting=all false, outflow=all false,
       leaves=nothing, comm=nothing)
Forest{T}(patches; ...)

patches is a vector (or tuple) of (roots = (…), extents = (…)), the extents defaulting as above, and glue a vector of (from = (patch, dim, side), to = (patch, dim, side), map = (…)), with side 1 for the low face and 2 for the high one. reflecting and outflow apply to the faces of every patch that are not glued.

Without leaves the forest starts as its unrefined roots. With it, it starts from that leaf list instead — keys in curve order, as forest.leaves holds them — which is copied rather than aliased, and the new forest is at generation 0. That is how a checkpoint package such as TreeIOHDF5 restores a forest. Everything built over a forest trusts its leaves, so the list is refused unless every key lies in the brick, the keys strictly increase, they tile the brick exactly — no gap, no overlap — and they are 2:1 balanced and, on a forest with a seam or glued faces, conforming there (see balance!).

comm is the communicator the forest is distributed over (M7), as communicator converts it; nothing, the default, is a serial forest. Every rank holds the whole forest, and the field data of a FieldSet over it are split by blockrange: each rank stores the blocks of one contiguous run of the leaves. Every forest mutation is then collective — the same call with the same arguments on every rank. See "Distributed meshes" in CODE.md.

Examples

julia> forest = Forest((2, 2); N = 8, periodic = (true, true));

julia> nleaves(forest)
4

julia> octant = Forest((1, 1, 1); N = 8, reflecting = ntuple(_ -> (true, false), 3));

julia> quadrant = Forest((2, 2, 1); N = 8, rotating = (1, 2),
                         reflecting = ((false, false), (false, false), (true, false)));

julia> star = Forest((1, 1, 1); N = 8, reflecting = ntuple(_ -> (true, false), 3),
                     outflow = ntuple(_ -> (false, true), 3));

julia> half = Forest((1, 2); N = 8, halfturn = (1, 2), extents = ((0, 1), (-1, 1)));

julia> next(p) = mod1(p + 1, 4);

julia> ring = Forest([(roots = (2, 1),) for p in 1:4]; N = 8,
                     glue = [g for p in 1:4 for g in
                             ((from = (p, 1, 2), to = (next(p), 1, 1), map = (1, 2)),
                              (from = (next(p), 1, 1), to = (p, 1, 2), map = (1, 2)))]);

julia> nleaves(ring), patch(ring, ring.leaves[end])
(8, 4)

TreeAMR.neighbor_keys — Function
neighbor_keys(forest, k::MortonKey, δ::NTuple) -> Vector{MortonKey}

The leaves abutting k across the face, edge, or corner in direction δ (see alldirections) — that is, the leaves that supply the data for that ghost region of k.

Valid on any forest, balanced or not. The result is

  • empty, at a non-periodic domain boundary;
  • one key, when the neighbor is at k's level or coarser;
  • otherwise every leaf touching the shared region, at whatever depth.

Under 2:1 balance (see balance!) the last case is exactly 2^(D - count(!=(0), δ)) keys, all one level finer.

Across a rotating seam (M12; see Forest) the keys are the real leaves whose image under the rotation abuts k, the leaves that supply that ghost region turned by a quarter turn or two. A block at the axis is then its own neighbor in three directions of the plane, as a single periodic root is its own neighbor. Across a half-turn seam (M14) they are the leaves on the same face, at the mirrored position along d2, turned by a half turn.

Note that this is not symmetric under δ -> -δ when levels differ: a coarse neighbor found across k's corner also spans the face beyond it, and reversing the direction from that larger block points elsewhere. Adjacency is still mutually discoverable, just not necessarily across the opposite direction.

TreeAMR.refine! — Function
refine!(forest, keys)

Replace each leaf in keys — a single MortonKey or a collection of them — by its 2^D children. Errors if any key is not currently a leaf, or is already at MAX_LEVEL.

Does not restore 2:1 balance; follow with balance!.

source
TreeAMR.coarsen! — Function
coarsen!(forest, keys)

Replace the 2^D children of each key in keys by that key itself. Errors unless every child of every key is currently a leaf — coarsening is all-or-nothing, matching refinement.

Does not restore 2:1 balance; follow with balance!.

source
TreeAMR.balance! — Function
balance!(forest)

Enforce 2:1 balance across every face, edge, and corner: refine any leaf that is more than one level coarser than a leaf it touches, repeating until the refinement stops rippling outward. On a forest with a rotating seam (M12) it also enforces conformity there: a leaf across a seam face that is coarser by any amount is refined, so the leaves on the low face of d1 and their images on the low face of d2 end at one level — or, across a half-turn seam (M14), their images on the same face. On a forest of patches (M17) it enforces conformity at every glued face in the same way, so no coarse-fine face crosses the glue; an edge or corner across a glue keeps the 2:1 rule.

Afterwards every ghost region of every block touches at most one level up or down, which is what bounds the ghost-filling cases and the prolongation stencils.

source

isbalanced(forest)

TreeAMR.block_origin — Function
block_origin([T], forest, k::MortonKey)

The physical position of the lower corner of block k's interior (the outer corner of its first interior cell, not that cell's center). On a forest of patches (M17) it is in the logical coordinates of k's patch.

It is a node's position, formed by nodeposition as lo + B·h from the patch's anchor lo (zero where zero is a node of the patch's level-0 grid, else its low corner; block_lows), the block's integer corner B counted from it and its level's spacing h, so that it is bit for bit the corner the blocks around it see and the first vertex coordinates gives.

source
TreeAMR.block_extent — Function
block_extent([T], forest, k::MortonKey)

The (lo, hi) physical extent of block k's interior, per dimension. Both ends are node positions (nodeposition), so the leaves' extents tile the domain bit for bit: a block's hi is its neighbour's lo, at one level or across two. The domain's faces are then at lo + K·h from the anchor, K an integer, which are the extents the forest was given up to rounding (CODE.md, "Positions" under "Centerings").

source
TreeAMR.block_spacings — Function
block_spacings(forest, T=floattype(forest))

The cell size of every block this rank stores, indexed by local block — what a kernel needs to scale a finite-difference stencil, since blocks at different levels have different spacings. Serially that is every leaf; over a distributed forest it is the leaves of blockrange, so that entry b belongs to block b of every FieldSet over forest.

source
TreeAMR.block_origins — Function
block_origins(forest, T=floattype(forest))

The lower corner of the interior of every block this rank stores, indexed by local block — the companion of block_spacings, for what in a kernel is not a point's position: a point is placed through nodeposition from block_lows and block_bases instead, which puts every copy of a point that several blocks hold at one position bit for bit. The corners themselves are formed by the same rule, so origins[b] is the position of block b's first vertex.

Together these arrays are the whole geometry a device-side kernel sees: plain isbits arrays indexed by block, no keys and no forest.

source
TreeAMR.block_lows — Function
block_lows(forest, T=floattype(forest))

The anchor of the patch (M17) of every block this rank stores, as an NTuple{D,T} indexed by local block, dimension by dimension: zero where zero is a node of the patch's level-0 grid, else the patch's low corner (the forest's own on a forest of one brick). The name is from when it was always the low corner. With block_bases and block_spacings it is what nodeposition forms a point's position from, lo + k·h with k an integer counted from the anchor, and why every block holding a point puts it at the same position bit for bit. A number per block rather than per patch, so that a kernel reads it as it reads the spacing.

source
TreeAMR.block_bases — Function
block_bases(forest)

The integer corner of every block this rank stores, as an NTuple{D,Int} indexed by local block: along each dimension the number of spacings of the block's level from its patch's low face to the block's low face. Owned index I of block b has the integer coordinate bases[b][d] + I − 1 there, which every block holding the point agrees on and which nodeposition turns into its position. Int on every backend, since a deep forest's coordinates overflow 32 bits.

source
TreeAMR.nodeposition — Function
nodeposition(lo, h, B::Integer, I::Integer, c::Integer, nodes)

The position along one dimension of owned index I of a block — the stored index minus the ghost width, so 1 … N (N + 1 on a vertex-like dimension's shared plane) for its own points and ≤ 0 or beyond for its ghosts — given its patch's anchor lo along that dimension (an entry of block_lows: zero where zero is on the patch's grid, else its low corner), its level's spacing h (of block_spacings) and its integer corner B (of block_bases). c is the set's stagger along the dimension, 1 vertex-like and 0 cell-like, and nodes is nothing for an equally spaced set, else the element's node offsets (a vector, or a tuple in a stencil).

The point's integer coordinate k = B + I − 1 counts spacings of its level from the patch's anchor, and the position is

fma(T(k), h, lo)            lo + k·h        (vertex-like)
fma(T(2k + 1), h/2, lo)     lo + (k + ½)·h  (cell-like)

each correctly rounded: the product is exact inside the fma and the sum is rounded once, wherever T(k) (or T(2k + 1)) is exact — k < 2⁵³ in Float64, 2²⁴ in Float32. The cell-like form takes the odd integer 2k + 1 and the half spacing, which is exact, rather than T(k) + ½, which loses the half one bit earlier. An explicit fma, not a multiply and an add, because a device may fuse the two of its own accord (CUDA does) and the host does not: with the fma written out, every backend rounds once and forms the same bits. Every block that holds a point at one level has the same k, lo and h, and a level up k halves and h doubles, both exactly, so every copy of a point — on a shared plane, in a ghost, a coarse point under a fine one — is at one position bit for bit, even where k is too large for the type to hold exactly. A type without an IEEE fma takes the product and then the sum (position_fma). This is the only function that forms a position from an index: coordinates, fill_by_coordinates!, the boundary hooks, firing_boxes and the folds all call it, and a kernel that forms a position of its own should too. See "Positions" under "Centerings" in CODE.md.

For an element set (M16) nodes are the node offsets in spacings from an element's low face, and owned point I is node i = (I-1) mod (P+1) of element q = (I-1) ÷ (P+1), at fma(T(B + q(P+1)) + oᵢ, h, lo): the offset is not an integer, so its sum with the integer part rounds once before the fma does, and the halving of the cell-like form would gain nothing. The first node (o₀ = 0) is the integer form exactly. The last node, whose offset is exactly P + 1, is formed as the next element's first, at fma(T(B + (q+1)(P+1)), h, lo), so that the two face nodes at one position have the same integer and get the same bits. A ghost of an element set sits at the face: lo + B·h below and lo + (B + N)·h above, by the same integer form.

TreeAMR.patch — Function
patch(forest, k::MortonKey)
patch(forest, root::Integer)

The patch (M17) that leaf k, or flat root index root, lies in: 1 on a forest of one brick, and otherwise the index into the list of patches the forest was built from. Patch extents may overlap or coincide, since the coordinates are logical patch by patch, so it is the patch number, not a position, that says which of the application's maps a block is under; see block_patches for every block at once.

TreeAMR.block_patches — Function
block_patches(forest)

The patch (M17; see patch) of every block this rank stores, as Int32, indexed by local block — the companion of block_origins and block_spacings for a forest of patches, whose extents may overlap or coincide: a block's origin then does not say which patch it is in, and so which of the application's maps it is under, and this does.
